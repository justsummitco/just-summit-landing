You leave a meeting knowing that something important was agreed. Ten minutes later, the exact task, owner or deadline has gone fuzzy.

That can feel like a personal failure. Usually, it is a capture-system failure. Meetings ask you to listen, respond, read the room, make decisions and plan the follow-up at the same time. An action item mentioned once—especially near the end—can disappear before it reaches your task list.

The [NHS describes adult ADHD](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/adhd-adults/) as sometimes involving distractibility, forgetfulness and difficulty organising time or finishing tasks. Experiences vary, and this article is general productivity guidance rather than healthcare advice or diagnosis.

## Why action items disappear

An action item has to pass through three stages before it becomes useful:

1. **Notice it.** Recognise that a comment has created a commitment.
2. **Capture it.** Record enough detail before the discussion moves on.
3. **Convert it.** Put it somewhere that can remind you what to do and when.

The failure can happen at any stage. Someone says, “Could you send that over?” but nobody names the file. A deadline is implied rather than stated. You write “send deck” but later cannot remember which deck, who needs it or why.

The answer is not to build a perfect transcript by hand. It is to give each action item a short, reliable path from conversation to task.

## Before the meeting: prepare one landing zone

Do not decide where to put the follow-up while the meeting is happening. Open one note before the call and give it four labels:

- **Decision** — what was agreed?
- **Owner** — who is responsible?
- **Deadline** — when is it needed?
- **Next action** — what visible step happens first?

If your team already has a shared notes page, use that. If not, a plain document is enough. The important part is having one predictable place rather than scattering fragments across chat, paper and several apps.

Add the meeting title and date at the top. If you are joining from another call, take thirty seconds to write the purpose of the meeting in one sentence. That small reset makes it easier to recognise which details matter.

## During the meeting: capture signals, not sentences

Trying to type everything can pull your attention away from the conversation. Instead, use four short markers:

- **D:** a decision
- **A:** an action
- **Q:** an unanswered question
- **W:** something you are waiting for

Your live notes might look like this:

- **D:** use the shorter pricing deck
- **A:** Tom send deck to Maya — Friday
- **Q:** does procurement need the security appendix?
- **W:** finance to confirm the final numbers

These are not finished tasks. They are retrieval cues: enough information to rebuild the useful context after the call.

When someone assigns work indirectly, pause and confirm it. A neutral question such as “Just to check, am I sending the pricing deck to Maya by Friday?” turns an implied request into a clear owner, object and deadline. That is useful for everyone in the room, not only people with ADHD.

## Use the final minute as a read-back

The end of a meeting is often where the most important details appear—and where attention is already shifting to the next thing.

If the setting allows it, use the final minute to read back the commitments:

> “I have Tom sending Maya the pricing deck by Friday, finance confirming the numbers tomorrow, and the security appendix still open. Is that right?”

This catches misunderstandings while the people who can correct them are still present. If you are not leading the meeting, send the same summary in chat immediately afterwards.

## After the meeting: run a five-minute conversion

Do not leave the markers sitting in your notes. Before opening email or joining the next task, convert them:

1. Put each action into your task manager.
2. Start the task with a verb: **send**, **review**, **ask**, **book** or **confirm**.
3. Add the person or outcome that makes the task recognisable.
4. Add the real deadline—or a date to clarify it.
5. Link back to the meeting note so the reason is recoverable.

“Send deck” becomes “Send the short pricing deck to Maya by Friday for the procurement budget review.” The second version gives your future self the object, recipient, deadline and reason.

If a task will take less than two minutes, you may choose to do it immediately. Otherwise, schedule it. Do not rely on the fact that it currently feels obvious.

## What to do when you have already forgotten

Sometimes the meeting has ended and the detail is already missing. Use this recovery order:

1. Check the calendar description and meeting chat.
2. Search your notes for the person, project or deadline rather than the exact wording.
3. Review any consented recording or transcript available to your team.
4. Ask a specific clarification question.

“What did I miss?” makes the other person reconstruct the whole meeting. “Was I sending the pricing deck or the security appendix to Maya, and was it due Friday?” is quicker to answer and shows the context you retained.

## Where audio recall can help

Sometimes the value is not just the action—it is the sentence that explains why the action matters. A consented audio-recall system can make that context easier to retrieve without turning the meeting itself into a typing exercise.

## The meeting-recall checklist

Before the meeting:

- Open one note with Decision, Owner, Deadline and Next action labels.
- Write the meeting purpose in one sentence.

During the meeting:

- Mark decisions, actions, questions and waiting items.
- Confirm implied assignments out loud.
- Capture the owner and deadline, not only the task.

After the meeting:

- Convert markers into verb-led tasks within five minutes.
- Add the recipient, reason and due date.
- Link the task back to its meeting context.
- Send a short read-back when ownership is unclear.

The goal is not perfect memory. It is a dependable recovery path—one that lets you stay involved in the conversation while giving important spoken details somewhere useful to land.
